Briefing

What is actually going on

Every trial plays 9–13 glyphs at a fixed 500 ms onset-to-onset rhythm, then a red dot tells you to predict. The stream comes from a Markov chain: each context allows exactly two successors with uneven odds, from 85/15 at the bottom of the ladder down to 65/35 near the top, and at the highest tiers the context becomes the last two glyphs rather than one. The chain is rebuilt for every run, so what improves is your ability to extract structure, not your memory of one particular chain.

Structure score is the honest measure: how much of the available predictive value your choices actually captured, where 0 is chance and 100 is the ceiling. Performance index is the overlap between your response distribution and the true one, reported the way the source papers report it. Strategy separates the two ways of being right — probability matching (answering 80/20 when the world is 80/20) versus maximising (always taking the likely one). XP and streaks are entertainment. They make a long run bearable; they are not a measure of anything, and a hot streak on a 20% branch is luck.

The evidence: Wang, Gates, Shen, Tino & Kourtzi (2023, Frontiers in Neuroscience, N = 105) found trial-by-trial feedback produced a 33.2 ± 3.6% improvement, block-level feedback 23.5 ± 3.9%, and no feedback at all still 19.0 ± 3.9%, with gains largely held four weeks on. Pushing the odds to 60/40 collapsed learning to 4.6 ± 2.2%.

What is not established is that any of this transfers to anything else. The Cambridge transfer trial (NCT05611788) is finished but unpublished as of September 2026, and the brain-training meta-analyses are blunt that far transfer sits near zero (Gobet & Sala 2023; Au et al. 2015, g = 0.13 and non-significant once active controls are required). This is a well-instrumented rebuild of a research task. It is not a device for raising your IQ, and nobody should sell it to you as one.
